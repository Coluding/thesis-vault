Lesson 01 · Parallel Decoding Distillation

The PD loss, and what we actually borrowed

One equation, one architecture trick, and an honest audit of the target already sitting in shortcut_targets.py.

~12 minD3decision-relevant

Why this one first. A PDD-inspired target is already implemented and a run is queued against the last of the compute. Before it burns, you should be able to say exactly which parts of PDD it contains. That is the whole win of this lesson.

1 · The one idea

Classical ODE sampling walks one interval per network call. PDD trains a network that, from a single call at state Xn, emits the mean velocities of the next L intervals at once — so one call advances L steps.

Everything else in the paper follows from making that one idea trainable and cheap.

The quantity being predicted

Not the instantaneous velocity v_t. The mean velocity over an interval:

Eq. 4 un(Xn) = 1/(tn+1 − tn) · ∫tntn+1 vt(Xt) dt This is the velocity that makes a single Euler-sized jump land exactly where fine integration lands: Xn+1 = Xn + (tn+1 − tn)·un(Xn) is exact, not an approximation (Eq. 3). The whole difficulty of few-step generation is that u is expensive to know and v is cheap.

The teacher supplies u by one solver step — Euler (1 evaluation, Eq. 5) or Midpoint (2 evaluations, Eq. 6). That is the entire teacher cost per loss term.

2 · The loss

Eq. 11 ℒPD(θ) = 𝔼 ‖ ūθn(k | Xn) − uk( sg( X̄k ) ) ‖² ūθn(k | Xn) — the student's prediction for interval k, produced by one call at Xn.
n — block start, sampled uniformly from {0, L, …, N−L}. k — an interval inside that block, sampled uniformly from {n, …, n+L−1}.
Xn ∼ ptn — drawn with the interpolant (Eq. 2), i.e. cheaply, from data.
X̄k — the state reached by following the student's own predictions from Xn (Eq. 9). This is the load-bearing symbol.
uk(·) — the teacher's mean velocity there, via one Runge–Kutta step. sg — stop-gradient.

Read it once more slowly. The student is asked: "from where you are now, what will the velocity be at interval k?" — and it is graded at the state its own predictions actually reach, not at a state some reference trajectory reaches. That is what "on-policy" means here, and it is the difference between a model that is accurate on the teacher's path and one that is accurate on the path it will actually walk at inference.

The paper's Proposition 1 states that the minimiser of Eq. 11 satisfies the parallel-decoder condition (Eq. 8) — i.e. up to the Runge–Kutta error, the student's trajectory is the teacher's, X̄n = Xn. No adversary, no score network, no JVP.

3 · The architecture trick

Given a teacher of the form v_t(x) = W·H_t(x) (Eq. 12) — backbone, then one final linear layer — PDD keeps the backbone and replicates only the final linear layer, once per grid point:

Eq. 13 ūθn(k | xn) = Wθk · Hθtn(xn) One shared backbone pass, N cheap heads (each initialised from the teacher's W). N is the grid size, not the block size — which is why one trained model serves many NFEs.

Two consequences worth internalising:

  • No second time coordinate. Flow-map and shortcut-style methods need to condition the network on both where you are and how far you're jumping. PDD indexes the jump by which head you read. The paper is explicit that this is the point: it "allows us to learn a single model that can predict any block size without the need to introduce a second time coordinate."
  • Free at inference. Generation only needs the block-average direction, so the heads collapse into one (Eq. 15): W_{n:n+L} = Σ_k Δ_k W_k, with Δ_k = (t_{k+1}−t_k)/(t_{n+L}−t_n). You store one fused layer per block. Zero extra inference compute versus the teacher.
Where "no JVPs" comes from. Learning a mean velocity usually forces you to regress a derivative of the velocity field — via a JVP or a finite difference, both expensive or unstable at video scale. PDD instead decomposes the mean velocity into L sub-interval predictions, and gradients through the shared backbone recover the full-interval signal in expectation. The derivative is never formed.

4 · What we actually borrowed

compute_teacher_rollout_target_v_flow in shortcut_targets.py is described in its own docstring as "the plug-and-play-adapter transposition" of PDD. That is fair — but the transposition drops three of the four load-bearing pieces. Being precise about which:

DimensionPDD (paper)Ours (teacher_rollout)Does it matter?
Target evaluated at X̄k — reached by the student's own steps (on-policy) States along a teacher rollout from xt (off-policy) Yes. This is the piece Eq. 11 is built around. Ours never composes student steps, so it is never graded on the states it will actually visit.
Teacher evaluations per loss 1 (Euler) or 2 (Midpoint) substeps=4, Euler Yes, on cost. We pay 2–4× PDD's teacher budget for a target PDD argues you don't need to resolve finely.
How the jump size is addressed Which head Wk you read Conditioning on log2(d) (use_step_level_conditioning) Yes, conceptually. This is exactly the second time coordinate PDD positions itself against.
Multiple velocities per forward pass L, by construction One Yes — "parallel decoding" is the name of the method, and we don't do it.
What is trained Full backbone, initialised from teacher Frozen base + small adapter By design — this is the thesis. But note Proposition 1 assumes the student can represent uk; a 34M adapter over a frozen base may not, and that is an open question, not a settled one.

The honest summary: what we implemented is mean-velocity regression against a finely-integrated teacher target. That is a real and defensible objective — it is not bootstrapped, so it cannot drift the way self-consistency can — but it is not PDD. It shares PDD's quantity (mean velocity, Eq. 4) and its spirit (regression, no JVP, no adversary), and none of its mechanism.

That is worth knowing before the queued run finishes, because it determines what you may claim: "a mean-velocity teacher-rollout target, in the spirit of PDD" is defensible; "we ran PDD" is not.

Free recall. Without looking up: write the PD loss and name every symbol. Then, in one sentence, say what would change in our implementation to make it genuinely on-policy.

ℒ_PD(θ) = E‖ ūθn(k|Xn) − uk(sg(X̄k)) ‖², with n a uniformly sampled block start, k a uniformly sampled interval inside the block, Xn from the interpolant, X̄k the state reached by the student's own predictions, uk the teacher's mean velocity from one RK step, sg the stop-gradient.

To make ours on-policy: instead of integrating the frozen base from xt to form the target, step the adapted model forward across part of the interval (detached) to reach an intermediate state, and evaluate the frozen base's single-step mean velocity there. Same teacher cost or less; different — and, per Eq. 11, more relevant — state distribution.
