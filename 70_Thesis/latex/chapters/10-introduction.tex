\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

A world model is a learned simulator: given a state and an action, it
predicts what happens next. If the prediction is accurate enough and cheap
enough, an agent can search over imagined futures instead of acting in the
world, which is the appeal of the idea and the reason it recurs.

Video generative models are an unlikely but attractive starting point for
building one. They are not trained to be simulators and they are not given
actions, yet in learning to produce plausible video at scale they acquire a
great deal of what a simulator needs: objects that persist, contacts that
resolve, motion that continues along its trajectory. A model that can
generate a convincing minute of a robot arm moving among objects has, in
some usable form, a prior over how such scenes evolve.

That prior is the asset, and it is expensive. The largest video models are
trained on data and compute a single project cannot reproduce, and a growing
number are released as weights that can be sampled but not retrained.

The premise of this thesis is therefore that the prior should be left alone.
What separates a video model from a world model is not physical
understanding, which the base already has in some measure, but the fact that
nothing in it responds to an action. The task is not to teach the model how
objects fall. It is to \emph{nudge} an existing competence in an
action-conditioned direction, with as little new machinery as possible, and
without disturbing what is already there.

We therefore ask what the smallest sufficient adaptation is, and what
governs whether it succeeds. Both halves matter: an adaptation that works
but requires retraining the base has given up the premise, and one that
leaves the base untouched but ignores the actions has given up the goal.
\citet{\nv{rigter2024avid}} have shown that the first half is achievable,
training an action-conditioned adapter on a frozen video diffusion model
using only the base's own predictions.

This thesis takes that result as its starting point and asks what governs
whether it works.

\section{Problem statement}
\label{sec:problem}

The question is not whether a frozen video model can be adapted into an
action-conditioned world model. That is established. The question is
\emph{when} the approach works, \emph{why} it stops working, and whether the
same composition can also absorb the second thing a world model needs, which
is the ability to roll out in few steps.

These are different questions from the one the literature has answered, and
they are the ones a practitioner faces. An adapter that improves every
quality metric on a frozen base may or may not be using the actions it is
given. An adapter that uses them on one backbone may fail on another for
reasons that have nothing to do with the backbone's capability. And an
adapter asked to learn two things at once may learn neither.

Answering them requires measuring something that is not normally measured.
The readouts used to monitor a conditional generative model, that is the
training loss, the composition weight, and perceptual sample quality, are
all insensitive to whether the conditioning is used at all. We show a case
in which they point the wrong way, and we develop the instruments needed to
tell the difference (\cref{sec:probes}).

\section{The arc}
\label{sec:arc}

The investigation is easiest to follow in the order it happened, because
each step was forced by the limitation of the one before it. We give that
chronology here; the chapters that follow are organised by deliverable and
report their evidence separately.

\paragraph{Adapting a frozen base works, and it is not our result.} The
starting point is \citet{\nv{rigter2024avid}}, who train an
action-conditioned adapter on a frozen video diffusion model using only the
base's own predictions. We reproduce that behaviour and find it robust: on
our data, the unmodified published recipe follows actions where three of our
own adapters did not (\cref{sec:res-working-cell}). Action conditioning on a
frozen video base is therefore not a gap in the literature, and this thesis
does not claim it.

\paragraph{A working world model is too slow to plan with.} Rollout cost is
linear in the number of sampling steps, and a planner evaluating candidate
action sequences pays that cost once per candidate per step of the search.
On the measurements in \cref{sec:res-d1} a single planning step on the
smaller of our two bases runs into hours at a realistic candidate count.
This is what makes sampling cost a research question here rather than an
engineering detail: it is the difference between a world model that can be
searched over and one that can only be watched.

\paragraph{What planning needs, and what the base has to supply.} Searching
over imagined futures imposes three requirements on the model at once, and
they are not independent. The horizon must be long enough that the futures
being compared differ in anything that matters; a window of about a second
bounds the planner regardless of how good the planner is. The predictions
must be accurate enough that candidate action sequences can be told apart at
all, which is a statement about the capability of the base. And the rollout
must be cheap enough to run hundreds of times, which is a statement about
the number of sampling steps.

The base we began from satisfies none of these comfortably. Moving to
Wan2.2 addresses all three: it is a more capable generator than the earlier
diffusion model, it trains and rolls out over substantially longer windows,
and being flow-based it is designed for short sampling schedules. A fourth
consideration is theoretical rather than practical: a straight interpolant
makes the published shortcut objective exact as written, which a curved
diffusion trajectory does not (\cref{sec:curvature}).

\emph{This move changes several things at once}, the training objective, the
capability of the base, and the usable horizon, and much of the empirical
work in this thesis consists of separating them again. We flag it here
because it is the confound that shapes the experimental design in
\cref{ch:experiments}: results that appear to be about flow matching may be
about capability, and the ablation exists to tell those apart.

\paragraph{Conditioning does not survive every injection site.} On the
stronger base the adapter improved the video and ignored the actions. It
took a purpose-built set of probes to establish that this was happening at
all, and a single-variable comparison to establish why: the same action
signal, supplied through a different injection site on the same backbone and
the same data, is used or discarded depending on how it enters relative to
the network's residual stream (\cref{sec:res-pathway}). This is the thesis's
central architectural finding.

\paragraph{Underneath the architecture is an objective that does not pay for
actions.} Even where the signal arrives intact, what the adapter extracts
from it is thin: a scalar summary of how much motion to produce rather than a
directional command (\cref{sec:res-decomposition}). The reason is economic.
Under a teacher-forced denoising objective the input already contains a
noised view of the answer, so the actions account for a very small fraction
of the loss at the margin, and appearance correction is always the cheaper
gradient. The solution that uses actions is substantially better on the same
objective; the difficulty is that the training landscape offers no visible
path to it (\cref{sec:boundary}).

\paragraph{The same economics predicts where acceleration can live.} If a
conditioning signal is already losing the competition for gradient, then
asking one adapter to learn conditioning and step-size behaviour together
should make it worse. We wrote that prediction down before running the
comparison, and organised the final experiments around three placements of
the few-step behaviour: in the adapter, in a second module on the base, or
in the base itself (\cref{sec:res-shortcut}). What the comparison shows is
that the two jobs have opposite requirements, which is where the thesis
ends.

\bigskip

The result is not a system that does everything at once. It is an account of
which parts compose and which compete, with the mechanism named in each
case, and an instrument for telling the difference that we did not have when
we started.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

Adapting a frozen video diffusion model into an action-conditioned world
model is not a gap in the literature: it is the contribution of
AVID~\citep{\nv{rigter2024avid}}, which trains an action-conditioned adapter
on a frozen base using only that base's predictions. We take that result as
our starting point. What is not established is \emph{when} the approach
works, \emph{why} it stops working, and whether the same composition can
carry a second conditioning variable (the sampling step size) that
would make the resulting world model fast enough to plan through. Those are
the questions this thesis answers.

\paragraph{A design principle for conditioning a frozen generative prior.}
Our central finding is architectural, and we state it as a design rule
rather than as a property of one system:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Conditioning must enter the network scale-free relative to the
residual stream.} Supplied as a per-frame modulation of \emph{normalised}
activations, an action signal survives and is used. Supplied as
cross-attention tokens, which add into an unnormalised residual stream,
the same signal survives the attention layers and is then attenuated to
irrelevance at the residual addition, at every scale and temporal alignment
we tested.
\end{quote}

We establish this causally rather than by correlation: a single-variable A/B
in which the two arms are matched on adapter contribution and on the
composition mask, so the injection pathway is the only difference
(\cref{sec:res-pathway}).

\emph{Scope of validity.} The principle is demonstrated on two backbone
families (a U-Net diffusion model and a rectified-flow DiT), at two
injection sites, for one conditioning modality (robot actions), on video
prediction. We do not test it on other modalities, on architectures without
a normalisation-modulation site, or at larger scales, and we do not claim it
transfers there without evidence.

\paragraph{A bound that architecture cannot remove.} The failures above are
mis-calibration and are fixable. Underneath them sits one that is not: under
a teacher-forced denoising objective the actions account for a very small
fraction of the loss on this data, so appearance correction is always the
cheaper gradient. This prices the conditioning out regardless of how it is
injected, and it bounds what any adapter of this family can achieve without
an objective that pays for actions (\cref{sec:res-decomposition}).

\paragraph{Step-size conditioning through the adapter.} AVID does not
address sampling cost. We extend the composition with a step-size argument
and train it with the shortcut self-consistency objective, so that few-step
behaviour is carried entirely by the adapter while the prior stays frozen.
This is unlike consistency and shortcut models, which retrain the prior. We
show
that this is learnable in the adapter setting, against a matched control, on
two independent statistics (\cref{sec:res-shortcut}), and we derive why the
published velocity-averaging target is exact only on a straight interpolant
and biased on a curved diffusion arc (\cref{sec:curvature}). Few-step
\emph{quality} is directionally better than the no-shortcut control but is
not competitive, and we scope it as future work.

\paragraph{A framework that makes the base a configuration choice.} If the
premise is that the prior should be reused rather than rebuilt, then the
prior one reuses will keep changing, and an adaptation method that is
expensive to port to a new base has not delivered on the premise. We
therefore implement the adapter taxonomy behind a single composition
interface spanning diffusion and flow matching, U-Net and DiT, so that
swapping the frozen base is a configuration change rather than an
engineering project.

The claim is demonstrated rather than asserted. The same interface runs
across several backbone families, and we added a new base family to the
original AVID repository as a third branch, running their recipe unchanged
(\cref{sec:res-d1}). We regard the resulting codebase as a contribution in
its own right: it is what allows the questions in this thesis to be asked of
a base other than the one we happened to start with, and what allows someone
else to ask them of a base that does not exist yet.

\paragraph{A diagnostic methodology, and the reason it was necessary.} None
of the above is visible in the readouts normally used to monitor such a
model. Loss, gate statistics, sample quality and perceptual metrics are all
blind to whether a conditioned model uses its conditioning. We exhibit a
cell that improves on every quality metric we record while carrying no
recoverable action information (\cref{sec:res-blindness}). We therefore
develop a probe suite that measures the \emph{structure} of the response
rather than its magnitude (\cref{sec:probes}). We regard this as a
contribution in its own right, and we report the case in which our own first
instrument misled us and what was required to correct it
(\cref{sec:integrity}).

\paragraph{What we do not claim.} We do not demonstrate trajectory control.
A paired shuffled-action comparison shows a modest, consistently-signed
effect on how much the arm moves per clip, but supplying the \emph{correct}
action does not reduce prediction error relative to a wrong one on any cell
we measure: what the adapter takes from the action is its magnitude, not its
direction (\cref{sec:res-decomposition}). We do not claim competitive few-step
video generation, a planning or reinforcement-learning contribution, or
state-of-the-art quality on any benchmark. Where a result rests on a single
seed, or on a comparison whose confound we could not remove, we say so at
that point in the text.

\section{Thesis structure}
\label{sec:structure}

\Cref{ch:related-work} places the work against adapter methods for large
pretrained models, action-conditioned video and world models, and the
few-step sampling literature. \Cref{ch:method} defines the composition
interface, the conditioning and step-size inputs, and the shortcut training
objective, and derives the curvature bias that motivates the choice of base.
\Cref{ch:experiments} describes the datasets, the backbones, the probe suite
on which every claim in this thesis depends, and the design of the ablation;
it closes with an account of the measurements that were invalidated during
the work and how they were caught. \Cref{ch:results} reports the findings, in
the order the argument needs rather than the order they were obtained.
\Cref{ch:discussion} states the boundary the results describe, the
limitations, and what would move them.

Readers interested only in the central architectural result may read
\cref{sec:probes}, then \cref{sec:res-pathway} and
\cref{sec:res-blindness}.
